# Which ramp should the robot take?

Research proposal · November 18, 2026 · Warehouse simulation

**Recommendation.** Use the two existing ramps and give the short route a loading-bay crossing with a variable wait. Run the same loaded delivery twice. When the crossing clears quickly, the robot should take the 14 m ramp. When a loading operation will occupy it for several minutes, the robot should take the 42 m bypass. Both routes must remain feasible in both demonstrations. Show the predicted cost before dispatch, the robot taking the selected ramp, and the energy the other choice consumed in a matched run.

Use the current served model to estimate the energy of driving each route. Extend the estimator to predict one quantity the current release has never learned, **how long the robot will wait when it reaches the crossing**. First establish in Gazebo that the lower-energy route actually changes. Invest in training and serving the extension only if that test passes.

## Why this warehouse needs a different operating condition

In the completed 121-mission experiment, the served XGBoost model reduced mission-energy mean absolute error by 96.8% relative to calibrated physics. Every planner selected the short ramp. Both ramps climb 0.20 m and the bypass adds 28 m of travel. At the maximum loaded mass, the gravitational work of that climb is about 4.2 J on either route. Changing payload within the qualified range alone offers little leverage against the energy cost of running the robot for several additional minutes. [Stage 4 results](validation/warehouse-energy/phase-4/phase-4-report.md) · [Qualified geometry and robot](../config/warehouse-energy/fixture-v1.json)

For the **0.50 kg uphill delivery at 0.10 m/s**, the two models give nearly identical estimates of the *difference* between routes, despite their different absolute accuracy. The forced-route data independently put the difference at 0.9152 Wh for that condition. ## What the research suggests

- **Predict delay at the place and time the robot will encounter it.** Yu and Wolf used warehouse traffic history and planned movements to predict future local delays, then incorporated the forecasts into route planning. For this demo, one crossing and a small set of dispatch-time observations are enough to test the principle on a single robot. Their result concerns fleet throughput. Our endpoint is modeled Wh per completed delivery. [Congestion Prediction for Large Fleets of Mobile Robots](https://cdn.amazon.science/8c/e5/80b4bc184aebb861fa764f8173cc/congestion-prediction-for-large-fleets-of-mobile-robots.pdf)
- **Keep uncertainty in the decision.** Street and colleagues used learned distributions of navigation duration to plan around congestion. Here, a route can have attractive average Wh and still risk missing a deadline. Estimate average wait for ranking and calibrate an upper allowance for the feasibility checks. [Congestion-Aware Policy Synthesis for Multi-Robot Systems](https://muehlig.eu/publications/paper-street-2021.pdf)
- **Evaluate the cost of the chosen route.** Elmachtoub and Grigas measure downstream decision error by the extra cost incurred when a prediction leads to a suboptimal choice. With only two ramps we can drive both under the same conditions and measure that cost directly. This makes route selection testable even when both estimators have small prediction errors. [Smart “Predict, then Optimize”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08005)

A second floor surface is a plausible alternative if the intended story is specifically about traction or grade. Quann and colleagues demonstrated terrain-conditioned path-energy prediction on a ground robot. In this warehouse, simply changing a friction coefficient does not establish a 0.915 Wh difference. A surface experiment should first bring the two corridor lengths closer, then verify a repeatable change in wheel work or traversal duration on forced runs with the same payload. A permanent ramp closure would make a good fallback demonstration, but only the open ramp would be eligible. The crossing experiment keeps the existing geometry and gives the selector two genuine options. [Terrain-conditioned path energy research](https://doi.org/10.1002/rob.21927) · [Prior warehouse proposal](warehouse-ramp-energy-navigation-proposal.md)

## Proposed implementation

1. **Make the wait observable in the simulator.** Represent a loading operation as an exclusive crossing on the A branch. At the existing level fork, an A-bound robot holds before entering the ramp until it receives a release. A B-bound robot can depart along its own branch. Drive the crossing from an independently seeded job queue with variable service times. Anchor the queue clock to mission dispatch and record entry, hold and release in simulation time. Keep telemetry, cancellation and RMF schedule updates active during a hold. A wait must appear in the wheel/auxiliary trace between dispatch and arrival. The current viewer’s pre-dispatch presentation pause falls outside that window. [Task runner](../scripts/warehouse-energy/navigation_trial.py) · [Warehouse adapter](../scripts/warehouse-energy/warehouse_adapter.py) · [Mission energy window](../scripts/warehouse-energy/run_stage4_trial.py)
2. **Extend the served estimator.** Reuse the v1 physics-plus-XGBoost driving estimate on the qualified segments. Train a small CPU XGBoost regressor for incremental wait seconds at arrival, including zero-wait examples. Inputs are queue length, active job class, elapsed service time, recent completed service times and planned arrival time at the crossing. Labels come from the independent hold/release events. Exclude scenario names, seeds, future releases and post-dispatch telemetry from model requests. Calculate route Wh as free-flow Wh plus predicted *additional* wait × idle W / 3,600. Add that wait once to the duration as well. Check stop/restart energy against traces and include a calibrated term if it affects ranking. Package both estimates in a versioned v2 release and recompute the uncertainty allowances on crossing missions. [Predictor](../apps/energy-predictor/predictor.py) · [OpenShift AI deployment renderer](../helm/warehouse-energy-demo/openshift-ai/render.py)
3. **Choose before dispatch.** Score both routes against one timestamped queue snapshot. Apply corridor availability, a 900 s one-way deadline and a 20% reserve on the 24 Wh battery after mission plus reachable charger access. Include possible crossing delay on the charger path at its own arrival time, or budget the qualified clear bypass. Use upper estimates for these checks and select the feasible route with lowest expected mission Wh. Within 0.01 Wh, prefer shorter duration, then route ID. The current Stage 4 selector logs the band but directly sorts by Wh, so implement and test the band for this extension. Pass the winning corridor to the existing RMF task and Nav2 map generator. Scope the first demo to one uphill decision per dispatch. [Route selector](../scripts/warehouse-energy/route_selector.py) · [Navigation generator](../scripts/warehouse-energy/build_navigation.py)
4. **Make each decision inspectable.** Record the queue snapshot, model version, both route forecasts, expected wait, Wh, upper allowance, ETA, reserve, feasibility and chosen route. Include context and snapshot freshness in cache validation. On an endpoint failure, score both routes with the same local physics and queue baseline and its calibrated upper allowance. For stale or missing queue data, use a qualified worst-case wait, take a feasible bypass or decline dispatch. Display the actual mode used. [Serving client](../scripts/warehouse-energy/energy_serving_client.py) · [Existing demo viewer](../scripts/warehouse-energy/demo_viewer.py)

## Experiment and decision gates

| Step | Work and decision |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Establish that a route switch is possible | Start with 12 forced missions, two routes × three actual wait regimes (short, near 330 s, long) × two repetitions, using the loaded uphill delivery. Replay the same job stream for both routes. Verify that each ramp wins away from the boundary, both remain feasible, and the Wh difference exceeds 0.01 Wh and the observed repeat variation. Inspect one complete stationary trace. If this fails, revisit the fixture before training. |
| 2. Learn and calibrate | Collect matched A/B runs from independent quiet, busy and near-boundary queue episodes with overlapping observable states. Include cases where a currently occupied crossing clears before arrival. Split by whole queue episode and job stream into training, development, calibration and sealed test sets. Keep both routes and all repeats from each episode together. Select the model on development-set route regret. Calibrate upper errors for total mission-plus-access Wh and deadline duration on separate episodes, including the routes the selector prefers. |
| 3. Compare policies on fresh episodes | Freeze the workload mix, features, models, thresholds and test size before revealing results. Use pilot variance to size the paired test for the 5% improvement target. Compare shortest feasible distance, calibrated physics plus a queue estimator, and the served model. Give all policies the same snapshot, telemetry assumptions and safety rules. Use a credible queue estimator based on job-class service averages, elapsed service, queue length and arrival time, fitted on the training episodes. If the real operation supplies a release schedule, include that rule too. |
| 4. Attribute and audit | Run v1 driving energy plus the baseline queue estimate as an additional comparison. This isolates how much the learned wait changes decisions while holding the drive model fixed. For each held-out episode, execute both forced corridors in randomized order from the same initial state and workload. Also dispatch live selected routes and correlate the decision with the RMF itinerary, Nav2 goals, physical track, elapsed seconds and integrated Wh. Retain rejections, fallbacks and failures in the report. |

**Success for the routing demo.** Identify held-out quiet and busy deliveries for which both ramps satisfy the constraints, the served selector chooses opposite ramps, and matched executions confirm each choice consumed less modeled Wh. Show near-ties separately. Report route-choice accuracy outside the tie band and mean **regret** (chosen Wh minus the best observed feasible Wh in the matched pair). Average across independent episodes, with repeats used to estimate simulator variation. With uncertain service times the per-episode winner is a hindsight benchmark. The planning objective is minimum expected energy among the feasible candidates.

**Success for an incremental AI claim.** On the frozen workload mix, target at least 5% lower mean modeled Wh than the queue-aware physics baseline, with a paired 95% confidence interval for the Wh reduction above zero. Report the distance comparison and the v1-plus-queue comparison separately. Retain the prior operational targets of at least 95% empirical upper-bound coverage, at most 10% mean completion-time increase against distance routing, complete agreement between selected and driven corridor, and zero observed reserve, deadline or motion violations on accepted missions. Report acceptance and completion rates alongside Wh so dropping expensive deliveries cannot create apparent savings. If a simple queue rule matches the learned policy, present the route switch as the value of energy-aware dispatch and report the measured incremental AI benefit, including a tie. ## What the audience sees

1. **Quiet crossing.** Display the same endpoints, 0.50 kg payload and starting charge, with both ramps available. Reveal the two forecasts, dispatch, and watch the robot take A in the existing noVNC view.
2. **Busy crossing.** Change only the loading workload. Show the dispatch-time facts and predicted wait alongside the break-even time. Reveal the selected corridor before motion and follow the robot up B. Use a preregistered scenario selection rule and label any accelerated playback.
3. **What would the other ramp have cost?** Put the replay of the matched A run beside the live result. Mark where the robot waited. Display predicted and observed Wh, stationary time, completion time, and the decisions from distance, queue-aware physics and AI. Label the alternate execution as a replay with the same workload. If AI and the queue rule differ on a held-out episode, show the queue information both received and the observed consequence of each choice. If they agree throughout, report that outcome. Finish with results across the full held-out mix so the audience can distinguish the example from the average.

**The first milestone is the crossing fixture and its 12-run feasibility report.** Proceed to model training when those runs show that each ramp consumes less energy under a recognizable warehouse condition and both can complete the delivery. All proposed savings refer to the modeled simulation battery.
